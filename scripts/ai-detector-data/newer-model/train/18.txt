Kyoto in the early morning is a different city. Before the tour buses arrive and the crowds fill the narrow streets of Higashiyama, there is a stillness that feels almost sacred. I learned this on my third day, when jet lag woke me at five and I decided to walk instead of lying awake.

The streets were nearly empty. A shopkeeper swept the stones outside her tea house, nodding as I passed. Steam rose from a small bakery where an elderly man was arranging trays of sweet bean buns. The wooden machiya houses, so crowded and photographed in the afternoon, looked quiet and dignified in the soft blue light.

I made my way up the slope toward Kiyomizu-dera just as the sun began to rise. From the temple's famous wooden stage, the whole city spread out below me, rooftops glowing pink and gold, mist still clinging to the hills. A monk rang a bell somewhere behind me, and the sound seemed to hang in the air for a long time.

I have traveled to many places, but few moments have stayed with me like that one. It reminded me that the best way to know a city is not always through its famous sights, but through its quiet hours—the ones that belong to the people who live there.

If you visit Kyoto, set an alarm. You won't regret it.
