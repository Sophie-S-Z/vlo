The old man at the end of the pier had been fishing for sixty years, and he had never once caught anything worth keeping.

At least, that's what he told the boy who sat down beside him one gray afternoon in March.

"Then why do you keep coming?" the boy asked. He was perhaps ten, with a runny nose and a jacket two sizes too big.

The old man considered the question for a while, watching his line drift in the slow current. Gulls wheeled overhead. Somewhere behind them, a radio played an old song through a café window.

"My wife used to ask me the same thing," he said finally. "Forty-two years, she asked me. And every time, I told her I was waiting for the big one."

"Did you ever catch it?"

The old man smiled—a small, private smile that seemed to belong to another time. "No," he said. "But I think I understand now that it was never really about the fish."

The boy frowned, clearly unsatisfied. The old man reached into his tackle box and handed him a spare rod.

"Here," he said. "Maybe you'll have better luck than me."

They sat together until the light began to fade, the boy chattering about school and his dog and the movie he wanted to see. Neither of them caught anything. And for the first time in a long while, the old man didn't mind at all.
