The lighthouse keeper's daughter had never seen the mainland, but she knew it by its lights. Every evening, when the fog lifted off the water, she climbed the spiral stairs and counted them: the harbor lamps of Port Elling, the red blink of the radio mast, the slow yellow sweep of cars along the coast road. Her father said there were eleven thousand people over there. She found that number impossible to hold in her head.

On the morning of her fourteenth birthday, a rowboat washed up on the shingle below the tower. It was empty except for a tin box wrapped in oilcloth. Inside she found a bundle of letters, all addressed to her mother, who had left the island when Mara was two.

She read the first one sitting on the cold rocks, the wind tugging at the pages. The handwriting was careful and small, the kind of writing a person uses when they want every word to count. "I think of you every time the beam crosses the water," it began. "I wonder if you think of me."

Mara looked up at the tower, at the great lens turning behind its glass. For years she had believed the light was for ships. Now she wondered if it had always been a kind of message, sent out into the dark by someone who did not know how else to speak.

She folded the letter and put it in her pocket. Then she began to walk back up the path, already deciding what she would say to her father, and what she would not.
