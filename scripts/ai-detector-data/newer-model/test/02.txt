Grief, I have learned, does not arrive all at once. It comes in small, unexpected moments: the second coffee cup I still take down from the shelf, the voicemail I cannot bring myself to delete, the way the house sounds different on Sunday mornings now that no one is humming in the kitchen.

My grandmother lived with us for the last six years of her life. She was not an easy woman. She had opinions about everything, from the correct way to fold towels to the moral failings of people who bought pre-sliced bread. But she was also the person who taught me to make dumplings, pinching the edges closed with her thin, quick fingers while she told me stories about the village where she grew up.

After the funeral, my mother and I stood in her room for a long time without speaking. Her slippers were still by the bed. Her reading glasses were folded on top of a library book she would never finish. It felt wrong to move anything, as if doing so would make her absence official.

What surprised me most was how much of her I found in myself. I catch myself saying her phrases. I fold the towels her way. Last week, I stood in the grocery store holding a loaf of sliced bread and heard her voice so clearly that I laughed out loud.

I do not think grief ever fully leaves. But I am beginning to understand that it is also a form of love, one that simply has nowhere else to go. And so I carry it with me, the way she carried that village with her across an ocean.
