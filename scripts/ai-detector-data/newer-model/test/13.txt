Autumn arrives quietly in the valley, the way an old friend lets themselves in without knocking. One morning the air is soft and green, and the next it carries a sharpness that makes you reach for a sweater before you are fully awake. The maples along the river are the first to turn, their leaves shifting from green to gold to a deep, burning red.

I have lived here for most of my life, and I still find the season breathtaking. There is something about the light in October, low and amber, that makes even ordinary things look precious: a wheelbarrow leaning against a fence, a cat asleep on a porch step, the steam rising from a mug of coffee held in both hands.

It is also a season of endings. The gardens are cleared, the boats are pulled from the water, and the long evenings begin to close in. My neighbor, who is eighty-seven, says autumn always makes her think of the people she has lost. But she says it with a smile, as if the memory itself is a kind of warmth.

Perhaps that is what autumn teaches us. Change is inevitable, and it is often beautiful. The trees do not cling to their leaves. They let them go, trusting that spring will come again. In a world that so often asks us to hold on tightly, there is a quiet wisdom in learning how to let go.
